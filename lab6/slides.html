Which Test?

Match the test to the variables

Outcome Groups Test Lab
Continuous (a mean) 2 groups t-test, t.test() 5
Continuous (a mean) 3+ groups ANOVA, aov() 6
Categorical (counts) categorical Chi-square, chisq.test() 6

Check-in: Do average commute times differ across four neighborhoods? Which test?

ANOVA. The outcome is a mean (continuous), and there are more than 2 groups.

Bridge from last week. Why not just run t-tests for every pair? With 3 groups that’s 3 tests, and each one has a 5% chance of a false positive, so the chance of at least one false alarm grows. ANOVA asks one question about all groups at once. Chi-square is for when both variables are categories, like survey answers.

Today’s Data

Same data, new groups

  • Same 380 MSAs as Lab 5, already joined: chainness_by_MSA_joined.csv
  • No column has 3+ groups, so we make one: split walkscore at the 33rd and 66th percentiles
chainness_joined <- chainness_joined |>
  mutate(walkability_level = cut(
    walkscore,
    breaks = quantile(walkscore, probs = c(0, 0.33, 0.66, 1)),
    labels = c("low-walkability", "middle-walkability", "high-walkability"),
    include.lowest = TRUE
  ))
  • quantile() finds the cut points; cut() puts each MSA in a bin

quantile() returns four numbers here: the minimum, the 33rd percentile, the 66th percentile, and the maximum. cut() uses them as bin edges. include.lowest = TRUE makes sure the MSA with the very lowest walk score lands in the first bin instead of becoming NA. The handout does the same thing to chain_pct to get chain_level for the chi-square test.

What cut() does

Figure 1

About a third of the MSAs in each group: 126 / 125 / 129

The dashed lines are the 33rd and 66th percentiles. Note the groups are equal in size, not equal in width: the high-walkability bin stretches all the way to 70. Turning a continuous variable into groups throws information away. We do it here to demo the tests, not because it’s the best analysis for this data.

ANOVA

Difference in means, 3+ groups

Figure 2

  • \(H_0\): \(\mu_{low} = \mu_{middle} = \mu_{high}\)
  • \(H_a\): at least one mean is different

Groups on the x-axis, a continuous outcome on the y-axis, the diamonds are the group means. H0 is that all three population means are equal. Ha is not “they’re all different”, it’s “at least one differs”. Keep that in mind, because it explains why we need a post-hoc test.

Running ANOVA

anova_result <- aov(chain_pct ~ walkability_level, data = chainness_joined)
summary(anova_result)
                   Df Sum Sq Mean Sq F value   Pr(>F)    
walkability_level   2 0.4647 0.23235   34.79 1.36e-14 ***
Residuals         377 2.5177 0.00668                     
---
Signif. codes:  0 '***' 0.001 '**' 0.01 '*' 0.05 '.' 0.1 ' ' 1
  • outcome ~ group: left of ~ is the dependent variable
  • \(p\) = Pr(>F) = 1.36e-14 < 0.05 -> reject \(H_0\)
  • But ANOVA doesn’t say which group is higher or lower

Read the formula as “chain_pct explained by walkability_level”. Df: 2 for the groups (3 minus 1), 377 for the residuals (380 minus 3). F compares the spread between group means to the spread within groups; big F means the group means are far apart relative to the noise. The p-value is what you report. And the punchline from Xiaofan’s slides: significant, but it doesn’t tell you which group is which. That’s the post-hoc test’s job.

Post-hoc: Tukey HSD

TukeyHSD(anova_result)
                                           diff         lwr         upr
middle-walkability-low-walkability  -0.04725876 -0.07153316 -0.02298436
high-walkability-low-walkability    -0.08525157 -0.10933631 -0.06116684
high-walkability-middle-walkability -0.03799281 -0.06212623 -0.01385939
                                           p adj
middle-walkability-low-walkability  0.0000187511
high-walkability-low-walkability    0.0000000000
high-walkability-middle-walkability 0.0007114593
  • diff: first group minus second (middle-low = middle \(-\) low)
  • lwr, upr: 95% CI of the difference; excludes 0 -> significant
  • p adj: \(p\)-value adjusted for running 3 comparisons

One row per pair. The name tells you the order: middle-walkability-low-walkability means middle minus low. So the -0.047 says middle-walkability MSAs average about 4.7 percentage points lower chain_pct than low-walkability MSAs. The CI runs from -7.2 to -2.3 points, which doesn’t include 0, so it’s significant. p adj is adjusted for doing several comparisons at once; it’s the probability of a difference at least this big if the two group means were actually equal.

Plotting the post-hoc test

Figure 3

  • Point = mean difference; bar = CI
  • Every bar is left of 0: higher walkability -> lower chain_pct, all 3 pairs significant

Same numbers as the table, easier to read. Negative means the first group is lower. So High - Low at about -0.085 means low-walkability MSAs have the higher percent chain. If a bar crossed the dashed line, that pair would not be significant. High vs Low is the biggest gap; High vs Middle is the smallest. The handout’s base R plot(tukey_result) shows the same thing, just harder to style.

Chi-square

Observed vs. expected counts

Observed (table())

              
               low-walkability middle-walkability high-walkability
  low-chain                 21                 43               62
  middle-chain              35                 43               47
  high-chain                70                 39               20

Expected if independent

              
               low-walkability middle-walkability high-walkability
  low-chain               41.8               41.4             42.8
  middle-chain            41.4               41.1             42.4
  high-chain              42.8               42.4             43.8

\[\chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O - E)^2}{E}\]

Big gaps between observed and expected -> big \(\chi^2\) -> small \(p\).

Chi-square works on counts, not means, so both variables are categories: chain_level and walkability_level. The left table is what we saw. The right table is what we’d expect if the two variables had nothing to do with each other: row total times column total divided by 380. Look at the corners: 70 high-chain, low-walkability MSAs where we’d expect about 43. The test adds up those gaps.

Running the chi-square test

contingency_table <- table(chainness_joined$chain_level, chainness_joined$walkability_level)
chisq.test(contingency_table)

    Pearson's Chi-squared test

data:  contingency_table
X-squared = 51.149, df = 4, p-value = 2.078e-10
  • \(H_0\): chain level and walkability level are independent
  • \(p\) = 2.078e-10 < 0.05 -> reject \(H_0\): they are associated
  • df = (3 rows \(-\) 1) \(\times\) (3 cols \(-\) 1) = 4
  • Again, it doesn’t say which cells are higher or lower than expected

Same pattern as ANOVA: one overall test, then a post-hoc test to see where the difference comes from. For chi-square, the post-hoc works cell by cell.

Heads-up: installing chisq.posthoc.test

The package was removed from CRAN this June, so install.packages("chisq.posthoc.test") fails. Install the archived version instead:

install.packages(
  "https://cran.r-project.org/src/contrib/Archive/chisq.posthoc.test/chisq.posthoc.test_0.1.2.tar.gz",
  repos = NULL, type = "source"
)
library(chisq.posthoc.test)

The error you’ll see with the normal install is “package is not available for this version of R”. The package itself still works fine; CRAN dropped it because the maintainer’s email bounced. The line above installs it from CRAN’s archive. It’s pure R, so no extra tools are needed.

Post-hoc: residuals

chisq.posthoc.test(contingency_table, method = "bonferroni")
     Dimension     Value low-walkability middle-walkability high-walkability
1    low-chain Residuals       -4.809442          0.3600946         4.424229
2    low-chain  p values        0.000014          1.0000000         0.000087
3 middle-chain Residuals       -1.495308          0.4372680         1.052772
4 middle-chain  p values        1.000000          1.0000000         1.000000
5   high-chain Residuals        6.265135         -0.7918547        -5.443081
6   high-chain  p values        0.000000          1.0000000         0.000000
  • Not a contingency table: no counts. Each cell has a residual and a \(p\)-value
  • Residual \(-\) = fewer than expected; \(+\) = more than expected
  • \(p\) < 0.05 -> that cell’s gap is significant (adjusted for 9 cells)

Each count in the contingency table becomes two rows here. Residuals are standardized, so bigger in absolute value means a bigger gap between observed and expected. Bonferroni adjusts the p-values because we’re testing 9 cells at once. Note this compares each cell to what we’d expect, not pairs of groups to each other.

Seeing the residuals

Figure 4

  • low-chain: fewer than expected in low-walkability (\(-4.8\)), more in high-walkability (\(+4.4\))
  • middle-chain: no significant gaps

Read across a row. Low-chain MSAs, the ones with more independent restaurants, are underrepresented among low-walkability MSAs and overrepresented among high-walkability ones; both p-values are tiny. The middle-walkability cell isn’t significant. High-chain is the mirror image. Middle-chain has no significant cells at all. This is the same story the ANOVA told: more walkable places, fewer chains.

Writing Up the Result

Four layers, again

  1. Report: \(F = 34.79\), \(p < 0.001\); Tukey: all 3 pairs significant
  2. Unpack: chain_pct = share of an MSA’s restaurants that belong to chains
  3. Statistical: reject \(H_0\); high-walkability MSAs average about 8.5 points lower chain_pct than low-walkability MSAs
  4. Context: what this means for independent food culture in walkable places

Same rubric as Lab 5. Aim for all four in Q1 and Q3.

Same structure as last week. For the chi-square, layer 1 is X-squared, df, and p; layer 3 is which cells are over- or underrepresented.

Three traps (the handout falls into them)

1. “Not due to random chance” -> say “unlikely to be due to chance alone”. A \(p\)-value can’t rule chance out.

2. “Influenced by” -> say “associated with”. Neither test shows cause; population density could drive both.

3. Read the numbers, not the sentence. The handout says high vs. middle is “the most significant” pair. It’s the smallest gap (\(-0.038\)). High vs. low is the biggest (\(-0.085\)).

These are the purple comments in the handout. First trap: a p-value tells you how surprising the data would be if H0 were true. It never proves the difference is real. Second: walkable MSAs differ from car-oriented ones in many ways at once, like density, income, and age of the city. Third: always check the write-up against the output. The Tukey table is right there.
