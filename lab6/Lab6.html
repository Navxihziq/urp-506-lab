ANOVA: Comparing Mean Difference between Three Groups or Above

In Lab 5, we learned to apply t-tests to see the statistical significance of mean difference between two groups. ANOVA is also used to compare mean difference but for more than two categorical groups. Let’s pick a different variable, walkscore, and explores its relationship with chain restaurants.

The goal of the following ANOVA analysis is to determine if there are statistically significant differences in the mean chain_pct between low-walkability, middle-walkability, and high-walkability MSAs.

ANOVA tests

The aov() function in R is used to perform analysis of variance (ANOVA). The aov() function fits an ANOVA model to the data, examining how the mean percentage of chain restaurants (chain_pct) varies across different levels of walkability (walkability_level). The variable to the left of ~ symbol is the dependent variable.

# Perform ANOVA
anova_result <- aov(chain_pct ~ walkability_level, data = chainness_joined)

# Display the summary of the ANOVA test
summary(anova_result)
##                    Df Sum Sq Mean Sq F value   Pr(>F)    
## walkability_level   2 0.4647 0.23235   34.79 1.36e-14 ***
## Residuals         377 2.5177 0.00668                     
## ---
## Signif. codes:  0 '***' 0.001 '**' 0.01 '*' 0.05 '.' 0.1 ' ' 1

Interpret ANOVA test results

The ANOVA results table provides the following key statistics. Among all the statistics, P-value (Pr(>F)) is the one used to most to determine the statistical significance of the test. * Df: The degrees of freedom for walkability_level is 2, indicating there are three levels of walkability (low, middle, and high). The degrees of freedom for Residuals is 377, representing the number of observations minus the number of groups.

  • Sum Sq (Sum of Squares): walkability_level: 0.4647 is the variability in the percentage of chain restaurants that can be explained by the walkability levels. Residuals: 2.5177 is the variability that cannot be explained by the walkability levels (i.e., the within-group variability).

  • Mean Sq (Mean Squares): walkability_level: 0.23235 is the average variability explained by the walkability levels. Residuals: 0.00668 is the average within-group variability.

  • F value: The F value of 34.79 indicates the ratio of the mean square of the walkability levels to the mean square of the residuals. A higher F value indicates that the variability between groups (walkability levels) is much larger than the variability within groups.

  • p-value: The p-value of 1.36e-14 is extremely small, far below the common significance level thresholds (e.g., 0.05, 0.01, 0.001). This indicates a very strong evidence against the null hypothesis.

Again, you don’t need to interpret every variable above in a statistical interpretation. The key one is p-value and it is often interpreted with original hypothesis in context.

For example, the ANOVA results show that there is a statistically significant difference in the percentage of chain restaurants among the different walkability levels. The extremely low p-value (1.36e-14) indicates that the differences observed are not due to random chance. Therefore, we reject the null hypothesis that the means of the percentage of chain restaurants are equal across the different levels of walkability.

[zhixuan’s comment]: careful with “the differences observed are not due to random chance” above. A p-value can’t prove that. It tells us that if the three group means were really equal, differences this large would almost never show up (p = 1.36e-14). So the safer wording is that the differences are very unlikely to be due to random chance alone.

ANOVA posthoc test: Tukey’s HSD test

While ANOVA determines whether the mean difference is statistically significant, further post-hoc analysis (such as Tukey’s HSD test) would be needed to determine which specific groups differ from each other. It is called a post-hoc analysis because you need to run the ANOVA model first.

# Perform Tukey's HSD test
tukey_result <- TukeyHSD(anova_result)

# Print the result
print(tukey_result)
##   Tukey multiple comparisons of means
##     95% family-wise confidence level

##                                         p adj
## middle-walkability-low-walkability  0.0000188
## high-walkability-low-walkability    0.0000000
## high-walkability-middle-walkability 0.0007115

The Tukey HSD test results are shown for pairwise comparisons between the levels of walkability_level. Here’s a breakdown of the first comparison (middle-walkability vs. low-walkability)

  • Difference (diff): -0.04725876. This indicates that the mean percentage of chain restaurants in middle-walkability areas is approximately 4.73 percentage points lower than in low-walkability areas.

  • Lower Bound (lwr): -0.07153316. The lower bound of the 95% confidence interval for the difference in means is approximately -7.15 percentage points.

  • Upper Bound (upr): -0.02298436. The upper bound of the 95% confidence interval for the difference in means is approximately -2.30 percentage points.

  • Adjusted p-value (p adj): 0.0000188. The p-value is very small, indicating that the difference in means is statistically significant at the 95% confidence level. The small p-value suggests that the observed difference is unlikely to be due to random chance.

In summary:

The mean percentage of chain restaurants decreases significantly as walkability increases, with middle-walkability areas averaging about 4.73 percentage points lower than low-walkability areas, high-walkability areas about 8.53 points lower than low-walkability areas, and high-walkability areas about 3.80 points lower than middle-walkability areas.

Contingency Table

A contingency table, also known as a cross-tabulation or crosstab, is a type of table that displays the frequency distribution of variables. It helps in examining the relationship between two or more categorical variables by showing how the categories of one variable are distributed across the categories of another variable. It can also be used directly as an input for the Chi-square tests below. Each cell in the table represents the count or frequency of observations that fall into the corresponding categories.

Our dependent variable, chain_pct is a continuous variable. To examine its relationship with other variables through contingency table and chi-square test, we will have to convert it into a categorical variable like walkability_level. Again, this is not the most effective technique for this dataset because our variables are mostly represented as continuous variables, but we will keep it simple for code demonstration. Chi-square tests are typically used for survey analysis because many survey questions are framed as categories.

Chi-square test

The Chi-square test is a statistical test used to determine if there is a significant association between two categorical variables. It is often used in conjunction with a contingency table to test the null hypothesis that the variables are independent, meaning there is no relationship between them.

  • Test Statistic: The Chi-square test statistic is calculated based on the difference between observed and expected frequencies in the contingency table. The formula is:

    \[ \chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i} \]

    where \(O_i\) is the observed frequency, and \(E_i\) is the expected frequency for each cell in the table.

When applied to the contingency table above, Chi-square tests can answer whether there is an association between MSAs’ level of percent chain and level of walkability.

# Perform Chi-square test on the contingency table; this table corresponds to `income_group`
chi_square_result <- chisq.test(contingency_table)

# Display the results of the Chi-square test
chi_square_result
## 
##  Pearson's Chi-squared test
## 
## data:  contingency_table
## X-squared = 51.149, df = 4, p-value = 2.078e-10

[zhixuan’s comment]: the comment in the chunk above says the table “corresponds to income_group”. That’s left over from an older version of this lab. contingency_table is chain_level by walkability_level.

Interpret Chi-square test results

Interpretations:

  • The X-squared statistic measures the difference between the observed frequencies in the contingency table and the frequencies expected if chain_level and walkability_level were independent.

  • The degrees of freedom for a chi-square test in a contingency table are calculated as (number of rows−1)×(number of columns−1). In this case, the degrees of freedom are 4, indicating the table likely has 3 rows and 3 columns

  • The p-value indicates the probability of observing a chi-squared statistic as extreme as 51.149, or more so, under the null hypothesis that chain_level and walkability_level are independent. This strongly suggests that the observed association is highly unlikely to be due to chance.

The chi-square test results indicate a highly significant association between chain_level and walkability_level. The extremely small p-value (2.078e-10) provides strong evidence against the null hypothesis of independence. This implies that the prevalence of chain restaurants is likely influenced by the walkability level of the area, or vice versa.

[zhixuan’s comment]: careful with “influenced by” above. A chi-square test only shows that the two variables are associated. It can’t tell us that walkability changes the chain level (or the other way around), since another factor, like population density, could drive both. “Associated with” is the safer wording.

Chi-square posthoc test

There is a post-hoc test for chi-square tests to determine which specific pairs of categories are significantly different. One common approach is to use pairwise comparisons with adjusted p-values to control for multiple comparisons, similar to what is done in ANOVA post-hoc tests. The R package chisq.posthoc.test provides a function chisq.posthoc.test() that perform posthoc analysis based on residuals of the chi-square test.

[zhixuan’s comment]: the text above says the posthoc test finds which “pairs of categories” differ, but chisq.posthoc.test() works cell by cell. For each cell in the contingency table, it checks whether the observed count is much higher or lower than expected (the residual), and adjusts the p-values for the 9 cells tested. That’s why the output below has a residual and a p-value for each chain level and walkability level combination, not one per pair.

# install.packages('chisq.posthoc.test')
library(chisq.posthoc.test)
# Perform pairwise comparisons with adjusted p-values through bonferroni method
posthoc_result <- chisq.posthoc.test(contingency_table, method = 'bonferroni')
posthoc_result

Let’s take the first row as an example for interpretation. The low-chain category is significantly underrepresented in low-walkability areas (negative residual -4.8 and very low p-value). It is significantly overrepresented in high-walkability areas (positive residual +4.4 and very low p-value). There is no significant difference for middle-walkability areas.

In summary, low-chain restaurants (i.e., independent restaurant) are less common in low-walkability areas and more common in high-walkability areas. Middle-chain restaurants are evenly distributed across all walkability levels. High-chain restaurants are more common in low-walkability areas and less common in high-walkability areas.
